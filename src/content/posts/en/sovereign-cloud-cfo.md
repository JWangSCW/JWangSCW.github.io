Ask ten people what "sovereign cloud" means and you will get eleven definitions. That ambiguity is expensive: when sovereignty is framed as a vague security concern, it loses to the budget committee every single time. Let me offer a framing that survives contact with a CFO.

## Sovereignty is a risk class, not a feature

A US-controlled cloud provider is legally required to comply with extraterritorial data access under laws like the Cloud Act. That is not an opinion about the provider — it is a structural fact. For most companies nothing happens, for years. Then one day a subsidiary, a client contract, or a regulator makes it matter.

Sovereign cloud buys one specific thing: **predictable legal exposure**. You can price that. You can insure against it. You can decide consciously how much of it you keep.

## The three questions that matter

When I present sovereignty to a decision-maker, I reduce it to three questions:

1. **Do we know where every byte of our data is?** If the answer is "I think so", the conversation is already won — for the right reasons.
2. **Could a foreign authority legally force access to our data?** This is a contract question, not a performance question. Read the terms your team clicked "accept" on.
3. **What is the actual cost of the alternative?** Sovereignty is not a blank cheque. European providers now compete on price; the right comparison is a portfolio-level TCO, not a sticker price.

## The honest arithmetic

The raw infrastructure price of a sovereign provider can be higher or lower than a hyperscaler — increasingly it is competitive. The real comparison includes the value of avoided risk:

- contracts lost because your client's procurement requires EU processing,
- legal fees and executive time if data access is ever contested,
- the strategic optionality of not being collateral in a geopolitical dispute.

When those items are priced, "we save 4% on compute" stops being a complete sentence.

## My recommendation

Do not run a "sovereignty project". Run a **data-residency and risk project** — properly scoped, with a decision matrix and a cost story — and let sovereignty be the answer that falls out of it. That is how it survives the CFO.
